This is a collection of European sources on insider risk. It sets out to cover the relevant open record in full, and each source carries a note saying what it establishes and what it does not cover, so that a reader can place it before opening it. A source qualifies if it is European in origin and if it sets out how a program is built, states a legal constraint on how one may operate, or reports research findings.

American material is not listed. Asked in English, the field answers in American, and that part of the record is gathered already while this part is not. The criterion is practical: what is collected here can be read in a European organization without first subtracting the assumptions that do not hold there. Several of these sources draw on American work themselves, and the line falls at the source rather than at its references.

The sources are grouped as guidance, law, and research. The guidance does not cite the law, and the law does not cite the guidance. The law is set out jurisdiction by jurisdiction, with a table at the head of that section giving, for each one, the provision that governs a measure of monitoring and what has to happen before it may be put in place. That table reports what the sources establish. It does not rank the jurisdictions, and it does not say what an employer should do.

## Program guidance
Summary: What a program should contain, from the bodies that publish on it.
Order: by date, most recent first. Undated and continuously updated sources come last.

### Insider Risk Mitigation Digital Learning {#npsa-digital-learning}
NPSA · United Kingdom · 28 July 2026
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/insider-risk-mitigation-digital-learning
Establishes: The mitigation framework taught in nine modules, with governance and leadership set as the foundation of personnel security.

Nine free modules of ten to twenty minutes each, structured around the Insider Risk Mitigation Framework and drawing on the authority's own research. The second module treats governance and leadership as the foundation of personnel security and introduces its seven key elements. It teaches the framework, and it does not address the legal conditions under which the measures it describes may be applied.

### Setting the Foundations: Five Principles for a Shared Approach to Insider Risk {#npsa-five-principles}
NPSA · United Kingdom · 2026
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance/setting-foundations-five-principles-shared-approach-insider-risk
Establishes: Five principles offered as a shared basis, with intentional and unintentional events placed on one spectrum of intent.

Sets out five principles intended to give organizations a shared basis for insider risk work, and restates the NPSA definitions to encourage consistent use of them. It treats intentional and unintentional insider events as both capable of causing harm, and places them on a spectrum of intent. It is a statement of principles rather than a method, and it does not address the legal conditions attached to any measure taken under them.

### NPSA Changes to Insider Risk Definitions {#npsa-definitions}
NPSA · United Kingdom · May 2023
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/blog/npsa-changes-insider-risk-definitions
Establishes: Revised definitions of insider, insider risk, insider threat, and insider event, organized around intent.

The UK national technical authority revised its definitions of insider, insider risk, insider threat, and insider event, and stated that it would use them across its own channels from May 2023. The reason given was that the terms had been used interchangeably and often in the wrong context. The taxonomy is organized around intent, and it does not address the legal conditions under which the corresponding monitoring may be carried out.

### Ongoing Personnel Security: A Good Practice Guide {#npsa-ongoing-persec}
NPSA · United Kingdom · 2023
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance/ongoing-personnel-security
Establishes: Good practice for personnel security through employment rather than at the door, holding that protective monitoring should be proportionate.

Collects guidance on managing personnel security through the course of employment rather than at recruitment, covering security culture, line management, access control, contracting, screening, reporting routes, protective monitoring, investigation, and exit. It is written as good practice for employers and assumes a United Kingdom setting. It holds that protective monitoring should be proportionate, without setting out the conditions that make it lawful in any particular jurisdiction.

### ENISA Threat Landscape 2020: Insider Threat {#enisa-2020}
ENISA · European Union · October 2020
https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/insider-threat
Establishes: Attack vectors, incident findings, and mitigation at the level of general control categories, in the agency's only thematic report on the subject.

One of twenty-two thematic reports in the 2020 Threat Landscape, covering the period from January 2019 to April 2020. It describes attack vectors, reports incident findings, and proposes mitigation at the level of general control categories. It is the only thematic report the European Union agency for cybersecurity has published on this subject, and it has not been updated since.

### Insider Threat Program Development Manual {#coess-manual}
CoESS, co-financed by the Internal Security Fund of the European Union · European Union · 2019
https://www.coess.org/projects-and-standards.php?page=insider-threat-project
Establishes: The structure of an insider program, as the closest thing to doctrine issued under European Union funding, unrevised since 2019.
Status: the manual is not downloadable from a European host. The copy that resolves is mirrored in the United States.

A manual for building an insider threat program, produced by the European confederation of private security employers and co-financed by the Internal Security Fund of the European Union. It is the closest thing to program doctrine issued under European Union funding, and it addresses the structure of a program rather than the legal conditions attached to one. It has not been revised since 2019.

### Insider Data Collection Study: Report of Main Findings {#npsa-data-collection}
NPSA, published as CPNI · United Kingdom · 2016
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/resources/insider-data-collection-study-report-main-findings
Establishes: How real insider acts occurred and what preceded them, from a review of United Kingdom cases.
Status: earlier editions of the same study were issued in 2009 and 2013.

Reports findings from a review of insider cases drawn from the United Kingdom public and private sectors, describing how the acts occurred and what preceded them. It is one of the few publicly available European evidence bases in this field built from real cases. The cases are national and now some years old, and the report describes what occurred rather than what should be done about it.

### Insider Risk Guidance {#npsa-guidance}
NPSA · United Kingdom · continuously updated
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance

The entry point to the national technical authority's insider risk material, from which the individual guidance documents, training modules, and case studies are reached. It is a navigation page rather than a document, and its contents change without a version history. Anything taken from it is better cited by the underlying document than by this address.

### Insider Risk Mitigation Framework {#npsa-framework}
NPSA · United Kingdom · undated
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance/insider-risk-mitigation-framework
Establishes: A structure for an insider program that an organization reviews itself against, with governance and culture as foundations rather than adjuncts.

The national technical authority's recommended structure for an insider threat program, presented as a set of components an organization can review itself against. Board engagement and governance, and security culture, appear as foundations rather than as adjuncts to detection. It is a structure rather than a method, and it carries no date or version, so a reader cannot tell when any element of it was last revised.

### Insider Risk Practitioners and Stakeholders {#npsa-stakeholders}
NPSA · United Kingdom · 22 April 2025
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance/insider-risk-practitioners-stakeholders
Establishes: That a program needs a senior stakeholder group drawn from named functions, and a director who carries the board's strategy into policy.

One of the three foundation components of the national technical authority's framework. It asks for a director to oversee the implementation of the board level strategy and to link it to policies across the organization, and for senior staff in each business area to hold responsibility for the risk assessment and for implementation in their own area. It then sets out the functions a senior stakeholder group, which it calls an insider risk working group, may draw on: physical security, information security, information technology, operational technology, human resources, vetting, facilities management, contract management, procurement, finance, counter fraud, legal, training, communications, and a staff or trade union representative. It names who is to be in the room and does not say what the room decides.

### Board Engagement and Governance {#npsa-board}
NPSA · United Kingdom · 22 April 2025
https://www.npsa.gov.uk/specialised-guidance/insider-risk-guidance/board-engagement-governance
Establishes: That one board member holds overall responsibility for protective security and is regularly engaged with the program, with a non-executive director as an independent champion.

The second of the framework's foundation components. It asks for visible board level support, for one board member to hold overall responsibility for protective security and to be regularly engaged with the people running the program, and for a non-executive director to act as an independent champion for personnel security. What it asks for is engagement, not a report of any particular kind: what reaches the board, and how often, is left to the organization.

### Help2Protect {#help2protect}
CoESS and partners, co-funded by the Internal Security Fund of the European Union · European Union · platform, undated
https://www.help2protect.info/
Establishes: Awareness and program-building material for critical infrastructure operators, with templates, and no legal basis stated for any of it.

An online training platform co-funded by the Internal Security Fund of the European Union, hosting a short awareness module and a longer sequence of modules with downloadable templates for building a program. It is addressed mainly to transport, energy, and other critical infrastructure operators. It is training material rather than doctrine, and it does not state a legal basis for the measures it describes.

### IT-Grundschutz-Kompendium, Edition 2023 {#bsi-grundschutz}
Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik · Germany · 2023
https://www.bsi.bund.de/DE/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Standards-und-Zertifizierung/IT-Grundschutz/IT-Grundschutz-Kompendium/IT-Grundschutz-Bausteine/Bausteine_Download_Edition_node.html
Establishes: What the German federal authority holds to be the state of the art, in numbered requirements, among them the checking of a candidate's own account of themselves, a logging infrastructure the administrators who run it cannot alter, a regular audit of the detection systems against the state they are supposed to be in, and how evidence is secured.
Status: a standard and not an act. Requirements carry three grades: a base grade the compendium says must be met first, a standard grade which together with the base grade it states as the state of the art, and a third offered as examples where the protection need is raised, settled by an individual risk analysis. Where a row rests on the third, it says so. Six building blocks are read here and the rest of the catalogue is not: ORP.2, ORP.4, OPS.1.1.5, DER.1, DER.2.1, and DER.2.2.

The compendium is organized as building blocks, each a subject with its threat situation and then its requirements, numbered and lettered by grade, written as obligations on named roles. Six of them bear on an insider program. ORP.2 Personal covers induction, departure, external personnel, and the checking of trustworthiness. ORP.4 Identitäts- und Berechtigungsmanagement covers how entitlements are granted, documented, and withdrawn. OPS.1.1.5 Protokollierung covers what is logged and how the logging is run, and it is the one place in the record that puts the logging infrastructure beyond the reach of the people who administer it. DER.1 covers the detection of security relevant events and the auditing of the systems that do it. DER.2.1 covers the handling of an incident, and DER.2.2 the preparation for forensic work before there is a case. It is written for the security of systems rather than for insider risk, and where it touches the person it does so through the personnel and the entitlement blocks.

### CyberFundamentals 2025, Essential {#be-cyfun}
Centre for Cybersecurity Belgium · Belgium · 1 October 2025
https://atwork.safeonweb.be/tools-resources/cyberfundamentals-framework
Establishes: What the Belgian centre sets out for the heaviest of its assurance levels, and the only place in this record where a national authority names the insider as something the detection tools are there to catch.
Status: a framework and not an act. It is published at four levels, Small, Basic, Important, and Essential, each a superset of the one before. What is read here is the Essential booklet, so a requirement recorded from it may not be asked at a lighter level.

The framework is organized in the five functions of govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover, each control written as an obligation followed by implementation guidance. Two of its controls speak to an insider program more directly than anything else in a national catalogue. The one on personnel puts the checking of a person's background alongside the access that person is to hold, and asks for it again at intervals. The one on monitoring end-user behaviour says in terms that what is being looked for may come from an attacker outside or from an insider, and names the classes of tool built for it.

### NSMs grunnprinsipper for IKT-sikkerhet 2.1 {#no-nsm}
Nasjonal sikkerhetsmyndighet · Norway · 2023
https://nsm.no/regelverk-og-hjelp/rad-og-anbefalinger/grunnprinsipper-for-ikt-sikkerhet-2-0/
Establishes: A set of principles for securing information systems, with the monitoring ones carrying what the collected data may be used for, what employees are to be told about it, and the requirement to verify that the collection is working.
Status: recommendations and not an act. They are written for any organization, and the authority says which of them are relevant will vary with its size.

Four groups of principles: identify and map, protect and maintain, detect, and handle and recover. The detection group is the substantial one for an insider program. It asks for a written strategy for security monitoring covering the purpose of the data, what is collected, secure storage including for legal proceedings, access to the collected data, the consolidation of logs, deletion, and a review interval. It says the workforce is to be told what is collected, what it is for, and how it is handled. It treats the normal state of the systems as something to be maintained rather than derived once, and says the maintenance has to answer to reorganizations, acquisitions, mergers, and downsizing.

### Guide d'hygiène informatique {#fr-anssi}
Agence nationale de la sécurité des systèmes d'information · France · September 2017
https://cyber.gouv.fr/publications/guide-dhygiene-informatique
Establishes: Forty two measures at a standard and a reinforced level, among them the joining, leaving, and function change procedures written with the human resources function, and a minimum retention of one year for security critical events.
Status: a guide and not an act. It is version 2.0 and dates from 2017, which is old for a technical document, and it is still the agency's hygiene guide.

Forty two measures across ten themes, each written at a standard level and, where there is more to ask, a reinforced one. Three of them bear on an insider program. The one on arrivals, departures, and changes of function asks that the procedures be defined together with the human resources function and lists what they have to reach. The one on privileged accounts asks for an exhaustive inventory kept current, and for a periodic review that names the working directories and the mailboxes of senior managers as what the review is for. The one on logging fixes a minimum retention and, at the reinforced level, asks for centralization so that an attacker cannot erase the traces of their passage.

### Baseline Informatiebeveiliging Overheid 2, versie 1.3 {#nl-bio2}
Nederlandse overheid · Netherlands · 9 January 2026
https://www.bio-overheid.nl/bio2/bio-producten/baseline-informatiebeveiliging-overheid-2-bio2/
Establishes: The government measures Dutch public bodies add on top of the two information security standards, among them a screening policy, a detection use case named in the text, and a log retention set against the scenario that an attacker has been inside for a long time.
Status: a baseline for Dutch government entities on a comply or explain footing, and not an act. It is an overlay: it is structured on NEN-EN-ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002 and states only what the government adds, so the greater part of what an entity owes sits in those two standards. They are not read here, and nothing is recorded from them.

The document is in two parts, a frame following the management system standard and a set of government measures following the controls standard, each measure numbered against the control it attaches to. Where the baseline adds nothing it says so and refers back. The measures it does add are concrete, and several of them are unusual in naming what is to be looked for rather than that something is to be looked for.

### Katakri 2020 {#fi-katakri}
Kansallinen turvallisuusviranomainen · Finland · 18 December 2020
https://um.fi/information-security-auditing-tool-for-authorities-katakri
Establishes: The criteria an authority audits against, among them the requirement to recognize which functions call for special trustworthiness, a clearance graded on three scales, and log retention set by the limitation periods of the criminal law.
Status: an auditing tool and not an act, and it is written for the protection of classified information rather than for information security generally. What it states is the level at which an authority will find a target organization adequate.

The criteria are in three subdivisions: security management, which carries the personnel requirements, physical security, and information assurance. It is used in facility security clearances and in the evaluation of the authorities' own systems. Its personnel criteria are the clearest statement in the record of screening graded by what the person will handle, and its traceability criterion the clearest of retention graded by what the records may later have to answer.

### Österreichisches Informationssicherheitshandbuch 4.4.0 {#at-ishb}
Bundeskanzleramt und A-SIT · Austria · 6 November 2023
https://www.sicherheitshandbuch.gv.at/downloads/sicherheitshandbuch.pdf
Establishes: That logging is only effective as a security measure once someone independent reads it, that where nobody independent can, the administrators' own activity is what stops being checkable, and that the evaluation goes before the data protection officer either way.
Status: a handbook and not an act. It is written for organizations and for public administration, and the sections that turn on Austrian law say so where they do.

Over seven hundred pages structured on the information security management standard. Its section on the control of log files is the one place in the record that treats the reading of the logs as a role rather than as a task, names the four eyes principle for the security critical cases, and sets out concrete criteria for what an evaluation looks for. Its section on the legal aspects of mail and internet logs states plainly that the monitoring of a worker's communications is a problem to which there is at present no clear solution, and settles nothing beyond describing where the competing claims lie.

### Regulation (EU) 2016/679, articles 5(2), 6, 12, 15, 35, and 88 {#gdpr}
European Parliament and Council · European Union · 27 April 2016
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng
Establishes: The grounds on which processing may rest, the burden of showing it, what a person asking has to be told and by when, when an impact assessment is owed, and the leave each member state has to set its own employment rule.

Article 6 sets out the lawful bases for processing, article 5(2) places on the controller the burden of demonstrating compliance, article 35 requires an impact assessment where processing is likely to result in a high risk, and article 88 allows member states to make more specific rules for the employment context. Article 12 sets the period inside which a person who asks has to be answered, one month from the request, extendable by two more where the request is complex or the requests are many, and then only if the extension and the reasons for it reach the person inside the first month. Where the controller will not act at all, the reasons and the routes to a complaint and to a court are owed inside that same month. Article 15 sets out what the answer holds: confirmation that processing is happening, the data themselves, and with them the purposes, the categories, the recipients, the period they will be kept, their source where they did not come from the person, and the existence of automated decision-making with meaningful information about the logic involved. That last provision is why the applicable rule differs between countries even though the Regulation itself does not. The Regulation does not address insider risk as such, and none of these articles names workplace monitoring as a category.

### Opinion 2/2017 on data processing at work (WP249) {#wp249}
Article 29 Data Protection Working Party · European Union · 8 June 2017
https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/article29/items/610169
Establishes: That consent is rarely a valid ground in employment, and how legitimate interests are weighed against privacy across nine scenarios of workplace monitoring.
Status: the Working Party was replaced by the European Data Protection Board in May 2018. The opinion has not been superseded by an equivalent Board instrument.

Assesses the balance between an employer's legitimate interests and an employee's expectation of privacy across nine scenarios, among them the monitoring of information technology use inside and outside the workplace. It holds that consent is unlikely to be a valid basis in an employment relationship, because the imbalance of power means it is rarely freely given. It was written under the previous directive and updates two earlier opinions, and no equivalent instrument has been issued under the Regulation to replace it.

### Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, article 5(1)(f), Annex III(4), and article 26(7) {#ai-act}
European Parliament and Council · European Union · 13 June 2024
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng
Establishes: That inferring emotions at work is prohibited, that employment and worker management are a high-risk category, and that representatives are told before deployment.
Status: in force 1 August 2024. The article 5 prohibitions apply from 2 February 2025. The Annex III high-risk obligations, which include category 4, were moved to 2 December 2027 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; the article 50 transparency duties kept their date of 2 August 2026.

Article 5(1)(f) prohibits inferring emotions in the workplace, subject to narrow medical and safety exceptions. Annex III places employment and worker management among the high-risk categories, which brings the full set of obligations with it, and article 26(7) requires a deployer to inform workers’ representatives before putting such a system into use. The Regulation classifies systems and assigns duties, and it does not say which detection methods are permissible under data protection or employment law.

### Directive (EU) 2022/2555, article 21 {#nis2}
European Parliament and Council · European Union · 14 December 2022
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2022/2555/oj
Establishes: That asset management, access control policies, human resources security, cryptography, and training are among the measures an entity in scope has to take, without saying what any of them holds.
Status: a directive, so what binds in a given state is that state's transposition of it. The deadline for transposing was 17 October 2024.

Article 21 requires essential and important entities to take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational, and organizational measures to manage the risks to the network and information systems they use, on an all-hazards approach, and lists ten things those measures have to include at least. Among them, at point (i), human resources security, access control policies and asset management; at (h), policies and procedures on the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption; at (g), basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training. It names the categories and leaves what they hold to the state of the art and to European and international standards. It reaches only entities in the sectors its annexes list, and it addresses the security of network and information systems rather than insider risk, so what it gives a program is an obligation to have these things and not an account of what they are.

### Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 {#nis2-ir}
European Commission · European Union · 17 October 2024
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2024/2690/oj
Establishes: What the directive's categories hold, in an annex setting out asset classification, the inventory, access control, privileged accounts, and removable media as requirements rather than as headings.
Status: it reaches the entity types its title lists, among them cloud, data centre, content delivery, managed service and managed security service providers, online marketplaces, search engines, social networking platforms, and trust service providers. Other entities in scope of the directive are not reached by it.

The directive names ten categories of measure and leaves what they hold to the state of the art. This regulation supplies the content for one class of entity. Its annex asks for a system of classification levels with every asset placed in one on confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity; a handling policy running from acquisition through use, storage, and transport to disposal, communicated to everyone who handles an asset; an inventory that is complete, accurate, up to date, and consistent, with changes to its entries recorded traceably; access control policies covering staff, visitors, suppliers, and service providers; access rights assigned and revoked on need to know, least privilege, and separation of duties, modified on termination or change of employment, authorized by the relevant persons, held in a register, and logged; a review of those rights at planned intervals, documented together with the changes it called for; separate policies for privileged and system administration accounts; and a policy on removable media. It addresses the security of network and information systems, so what it gives an insider program is the shape of the controls that program leans on rather than anything about insider risk itself.

### NTH Haustechnik GmbH v EM {#nth-haustechnik}
Court of Justice of the European Union, Fifth Chamber · European Union · Case C-484/24, on a reference from the Landesarbeitsgericht Niedersachsen · 18 June 2026
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A62024CJ0484
Establishes: That a court may rely on data obtained in breach, subject to minimization of what it admits and to considering anonymization before disclosure.

The Court held that a national court may rely on personal data that a party obtained in breach of the Regulation, and that a failure to inform the data subject does not by itself bar the use of that data in the proceedings. It requires the court to apply data minimization to what it admits, confining the material to what is adequate, relevant, and necessary, and to consider anonymization or pseudonymization before disclosing it to other parties. It decides what a court may do with data already gathered, and not whether the gathering was lawful.

### Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, amending the Artificial Intelligence Act {#digital-omnibus-ai}
European Parliament and Council · European Union · 8 July 2026
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng
Establishes: When the high-risk obligations begin to apply, moved to December 2027, without altering what they classify.

Moves the application of the Annex III high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and the obligations for artificial intelligence embedded in products already covered by product safety law to 2 August 2028. The transparency duties of article 50 and the prohibited practices of article 5 keep their original dates. It changes when the classifications bite and not what they classify, so recruitment and worker management remain a high-risk category throughout.

### Bărbulescu v. Romania {#barbulescu}
European Court of Human Rights, Grand Chamber · Application no. 61496/08 · 5 September 2017
https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-177082
Establishes: That private life and correspondence reach into the workplace, and the six criteria against which any monitoring measure is weighed.
Jurisdiction: Council of Europe

The Grand Chamber held that private life and correspondence extend into the workplace, including where an employee's private use of a work device breaches the employer's rules. It set out six criteria against which a monitoring measure is to be assessed, among them whether the employee was notified in advance and whether a less intrusive method would have served. It does not hold that workplace monitoring is unlawful, and it decides against the domestic courts for failing to weigh these matters rather than against the employer.

### López Ribalda and Others v. Spain {#lopez-ribalda}
European Court of Human Rights, Grand Chamber · Applications nos. 1874/13 and 8567/13 · 17 October 2019
https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-197098
Establishes: That those criteria are weighed rather than counted, and that surveillance without notice can survive where a suspicion preceded it.
Jurisdiction: Council of Europe
Status: this is the Grand Chamber judgment. It reversed the Chamber judgment of 9 January 2018, which is a different document and is the one a search commonly returns first.

The Grand Chamber found no violation of article 8 where a retailer installed hidden cameras after suspecting theft, reversing a Chamber judgment that had found one. It applied the criteria from Bărbulescu and accepted the measure even though the employees had not been notified, on the basis of a prior reasonable suspicion and the limited duration and scope of the surveillance. It establishes that the criteria are weighed rather than counted, and that failing one of them does not decide the outcome.

### Florindo de Almeida Vasconcelos Gramaxo v. Portugal {#gramaxo}
European Court of Human Rights, Fourth Section · Application no. 26968/16 · 13 December 2022
https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-221932
Establishes: That geolocation of a company vehicle, inside and outside working hours, did not violate article 8 on the facts, by four votes to three.
Jurisdiction: Council of Europe

## What does not exist (yet)
Summary: The holes in the record, stated rather than left to be discovered.

There is no European insider symposium. The reference event in the field is American, in its eleventh edition, held in Washington.

There is no European professional body for the discipline.
